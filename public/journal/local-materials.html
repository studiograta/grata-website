Local is a supply chain, not a mood

Regional materials can give a resort its sense of place. They can also delay its opening. The difference is in how you source them.

Amritha Madhavan

Almost every resort brief we see asks for “local”. Athangudi tiles on the verandah, cane in the lounge, handloom on the beds, laterite in the walls. It's the right instinct. Guests can tell a place that belongs to its landscape from one that could be anywhere.

But “local” on a moodboard and “local” in a 40-key resort are two different things. The first is a feeling. The second is a supply chain, and it has to be planned like one.

Start with capacity, not the catalogue

Most regional crafts are made by family workshops and small units. Many of them are set up to supply homes and small commercial jobs, not a few thousand matching pieces on a fixed deadline.

So the first question isn't “do we like it?” It's “can they make enough of it, in time?” We ask every workshop three things:

  • How much do you make in a month?
  • What else is on your order book for those months?
  • Who else can make this if you fall short?

If the answers don't add up, we don't drop the material. We change where it goes: a feature wall instead of every floor, the lounge instead of every room.

Variation is the point, so plan for it

A handmade tile is cured by hand and dried in the open. A cane panel depends on the weaver and the batch of cane. That variation is a big part of why these materials feel alive. In a hotel it can also look like a mistake if it isn't managed.

What we do:

  • Approve a production sample, not a showroom piece. The piece in the showroom was chosen because it's the best one.
  • Set a range, not a single swatch. We agree with the maker what “acceptable” looks like at both ends, light and dark.
  • Blend on site. Tiles from different boxes are mixed before laying, so variation reads as texture rather than patches.

Seasons set the calendar

Many traditional materials run on weather. Cement tiles and lime need curing time. Timber needs seasoning. Open-air drying slows down in the monsoon. Artisan teams get smaller around harvests and festivals.

A resort opening calendar has to work around these, not against them. That's why regional materials get the earliest freeze points on our projects. If a material needs months, the decision needs to happen months before that.

Not every local material belongs everywhere

Being rooted in a place doesn't make a material right for every part of a hotel. A few honest limits:

  • Some handmade cement tiles stain where oil and water spill. That makes them better suited to lobbies and verandahs than to kitchens or busy wet areas, unless the finish and maintenance are planned for it.
  • Natural cane and rattan need treatment and the right placement in humid, coastal sites.
  • Handloom fabrics are beautiful for throws, cushions and curtains. High-wear seating usually needs a tougher, contract-grade textile.

The aim is to put each material where it does its best work and lasts.

Paperwork and packing are part of the material

Two things owners often find out late:

  • Invoicing. Smaller units don't always invoice in the format a hotel's accounts team needs. Sort this out before the order, not at payment.
  • Breakage. Handmade pieces travelling across states need proper crating. Plan for transit losses in the order quantity.

This is also where attic stock matters most. If a handmade tile cracks two years in, you may not get the same batch, or even the same workshop, again. Buy your spares with the main order. (We've written about this separately in Attic stock: the line item nobody budgets.)

How we work

On every project we draw on our trade network and finish library: workshops we've visited and materials we've seen in use, not just in a sample box. Where volume is a risk, we split orders across two makers with a shared reference sample. And we write the regional materials into the delivery plan from day one, so the sense of place doesn't become the reason the opening slips.

Questions to ask before you commit to a regional material

  1. Has someone visited the workshop and seen its capacity?
  2. Is there an approved production sample and an agreed range?
  3. Is the order sized for transit losses and attic stock?
  4. Does the calendar allow for curing, seasoning and weather?
  5. Is each material placed where it will hold up to hotel use?

Done well, local is the most memorable thing in the building. Done as a mood, it's usually the last thing to arrive on site.
