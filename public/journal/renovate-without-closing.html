Renovate without closing

Every week a space is closed is revenue that doesn't come back. Plan the work around the business, not the other way round.

Ask an owner why they've put off a renovation and the answer is rarely the cost of the work. It's the cost of closing. Rent, salaries and loan payments keep running. Regulars find somewhere else. Staff you trained for years take other jobs.

Closing completely is sometimes the right call. But it should be a decision, not a default. Most renovations can be planned so that part of the space keeps earning while the rest is rebuilt.

Put a number on closing

Before choosing how to phase the work, work out what a week closed actually costs. Take the revenue you'd lose, add the costs that keep running, and add a little for the guests who won't come back. Then compare it with the cost of phasing: a longer programme, night shifts, temporary partitions and some lost covers or rooms along the way.

Often phasing wins. Sometimes a short, sharp closure in the quietest month is cheaper. Either way, you're now choosing with numbers.

Ways to keep earning

Work zone by zone

Split the space into zones that can be closed one at a time. A hotel takes one floor or wing out of inventory. A restaurant closes the private dining room first, then half the main floor. Each zone is finished and handed back before the next one starts.

Work when you're closed anyway

A restaurant that opens at noon has its mornings. A bar has its days. Noisy and dusty work, like demolition, cutting and sanding, goes into those hours. Quiet work, like painting and fixing, can happen closer to service.

Use the quiet season

Resorts and hill-station hotels have months when occupancy drops. Plan the heaviest work for that window, and have every material on site before it opens. A delivery that arrives three weeks late can push the work into peak season.

Keep the core running

Some parts of the business can't stop: the kitchen, the front desk, the toilets, the fire exits. Plan these first.

  • Kitchens and bars: if they must move, set up a temporary line before the old one is touched.
  • Services: plan any shutdown of water, power or gas for the hours you're closed, and tell the team in advance.
  • Routes: keep guest routes and fire exits clear and separate from the site. Guests should never walk past a skip.

Protect the guest experience

A guest paying full price doesn't want to hear a drill. Phasing only works if the open part of the space still feels right.

  • Use solid, finished-looking hoardings, not plastic sheeting. They can even show what's coming.
  • Seal against dust, and run noisy work outside service hours.
  • Tell guests before they book. A small discount or a note of thanks earns more goodwill than silence.
  • Brief your staff, so they can answer questions with confidence.
The cost of phasing Working in phases usually takes longer and costs a little more in labour. It pays back when it keeps more revenue coming in than it adds.

Design for it from the start

Phasing isn't something to add once the design is done. It changes the design. Materials with long lead times get ordered early. Prefabricated joinery, made off site and fitted in days, cuts the time each zone is closed. Finishes that go on quickly and dry fast keep zones out of action for less time.

We draw the phasing plan alongside the layout, so every zone has a start date, a finish date and a route around it.

Five questions for your contractor and designer

  1. What does each week closed cost us, and how does phasing compare?
  2. How will the work be split into zones, and in what order?
  3. What will guests see, hear and smell while the work is on?
  4. Which materials have the longest lead times, and are they ordered?
  5. What happens to the programme if something arrives late?

A renovation that keeps the doors open is planned twice: once for the space, and once for the business inside it.
