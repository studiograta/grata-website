Fit out for the team you'll be in 18 months

A growing company can outgrow its office before the fit-out has paid for itself.

date
3 November 2026
read
5 min
by
Shifaz Salim

Most founders sign an office lease for the team they have today. Eighteen months later the team has doubled, or a round has slipped and it has shrunk, and the office is the wrong size. The desks can move. Most of what you paid to build cannot.

The fit-out is one of the largest cheques a young company writes, and one of the few it can't take with it. So it is worth designing for the team you expect to become, not just the one you have.

What a fit-out costs, and what it costs to leave

Cushman & Wakefield's 2026 fit-out cost guide puts a collaborative hybrid office in Bengaluru at about ₹6,027 per sq ft. It also prices reinstatement, the work of returning the space to the landlord's condition when you leave, at about ₹789 per sq ft.

That second number is the one founders forget. You pay to build the office, and then you pay again to take it apart.

A worked example

A 40-person team takes 3,000 sq ft on a three-year lease:

  • Fit-out: 3,000 × ₹6,027 = about ₹1.8 crore
  • Reinstatement at exit: 3,000 × ₹789 = about ₹24 lakh

Spread over the full three years, the fit-out costs about ₹12,600 per seat per month, before rent. If the team outgrows the space at 18 months, that doubles to about ₹25,100 per seat per month, with the reinstatement bill still to come.

When flex space is the better answer

Plenty of companies have done this sum and chosen not to fit out at all. Cushman & Wakefield counted 191,306 flex seats leased across India's top eight cities in the first half of 2026, up 68% on a year earlier. Bengaluru was the largest market, with 57,487 seats, or 30% of the total.

A managed or coworking office makes sense when your headcount could swing by a third either way, when you need to be in within weeks, or when your team mostly needs desks and a few rooms. It makes less sense when clients visit often, when your team has needs a standard floor can't meet, or when you are confident of the space for three years or more.

Many teams do both: a flex floor for the team that's still growing, and a small space of their own, built properly, for clients and the people who are in every day.

If you fit out, design it to move

The decision isn't all or nothing. Some of an office has to be built in. A lot of it doesn't.

  • Build the base for change. Lay out lighting, air-conditioning and floor power on a regular grid, so a desk area can become a meeting room without rewiring the floor.
  • Keep furniture loose. Desks, storage and meeting tables that aren't fixed to the floor can be rearranged on a weekend and moved to the next office.
  • Use partitions you can take down. Demountable glass and panel walls cost more than drywall on day one, and far less when you need to change the plan.
  • Make the front of house portable. The reception counter, the client room furniture, the lighting and the signage are what a visitor remembers. Build them as pieces that can travel, and your next office starts with them.

The four numbers, for a growing team

We judge every space by four numbers. For a growing company, each one has a time attached.

  • Capacity: how many it holds at peak today, and how many it can hold in 18 months without a rebuild.
  • Value: what a visiting client or candidate sees, and whether that comes with you when you move.
  • Flow: how quickly a room or a zone can be reset for a new use.
  • Upkeep: how it ages, and what it will cost to return the space at the end of the lease.

Five questions before you sign

  1. What is your headcount plan for 12, 18 and 36 months, and what happens if it is wrong by a third?
  2. How long is the lock-in, and how does it compare with how long you expect to fit?
  3. What does the lease say you must reinstate when you leave?
  4. Which parts of this fit-out could you take to your next office?
  5. Does a client need to see this office, or just your team?
