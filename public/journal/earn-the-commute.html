The office has to earn the commute

You can ask your team to come in three days a week. The room decides whether those days are worth it.

date
3 November 2026
read
6 min
by
Shirin KC

The office day policy is settled. In JLL's 2025 survey, 82% of employees in India said their company sets a number of days they must be in the office, well above the Asia Pacific average of 71%. Most of them were fine with it. The harder question for a founder is the next one: when the whole team turns up on Tuesday, does the office give them a reason to have come?

Too often it doesn't. There is nowhere to take a call, the one big meeting room is holding a conversation for two, and the people who came in to get work done spend the day with headphones on. Here is what the research says people come in for, and how to design for it.

What people come in for

Gensler's 2025 Global Workplace Survey asked more than 16,800 office workers in 15 countries, India among them, why they come to the office. Focusing on their own work ranked among the top reasons. So did scheduled meetings with their team, socialising with colleagues and sitting with their team. Three of the top five reasons were about being with other people.

The same survey found that only 26% of workers strongly agree that their workplace helps them do their best work. People are back. The rooms have not caught up.

So the office has two jobs on the same day: somewhere to be together, and somewhere to concentrate. Most startup offices are planned for neither. They are planned for a headcount.

1. Capacity: plan for Tuesday, not the average

A team with set office days does not spread itself evenly across the week. The same two or three days fill up and the rest are quiet. An office sized on the average day feels empty on Friday and overflows on Tuesday, and Tuesday is the day that shapes how people feel about coming in.

The design question: on your busiest day, how many people are in, how many are on calls at once, and how many meetings are running at the same time? We plan desks, rooms and call spaces around that day, then make sure the quieter days still feel lived in rather than abandoned.

2. Flow: small rooms, and plenty of them

When Gensler asked people how they choose where to work with colleagues, availability came first (44%), ahead of comfort, acoustic privacy and location. People use the room that is free, not the room that suits the task.

And rooms are often not free for the reason you'd think. Density, a company that sells room-use sensors, measured meeting rooms in two offices of a large US firm over four months in 2023. In one office 24.5% of booked meetings never happened, and in the other 28.1%. Rooms for 6 to 16 people held fewer than three people on average, and six-person rooms were used by one or two people more than 70% of the time.

The pattern is common: one boardroom that is always booked, used mostly for small conversations. For a growing team, several rooms for two to four people, plus a couple of phone booths, usually do more work than one big table.

The design question: how fast can someone find a free room and start talking? Rooms you can see into from the desks, a booking system that releases no-shows, and furniture that needs no rearranging all keep rooms turning.

3. Quiet is a layout decision

Open plans are meant to bring people together, but they don't always. When Harvard researchers Ethan Bernstein and Stephen Turban tracked employees at two companies moving into open offices, face-to-face interaction fell by about 70%, and email and messaging rose. People who can't find quiet create it with headphones and stop talking altogether.

In Gensler's survey, the most common distraction at desks was a colleague's conversation, on a call or in person. In meeting rooms, people complained about being overheard and about noise from outside.

The design question: where does the noise go? Put the busy things (the entrance, the pantry, the team tables) together, and the quiet desks and focus rooms away from them. Give meeting rooms walls and doors that actually stop sound. Treat ceilings and floors, not just walls. These decisions cost little at drawing stage and a great deal once the team has moved in.

4. Value: a reason to make the trip

Gensler found that only 14% of employees want a "corporate" office. Far more were drawn to spaces that feel calm, natural and easy, and 40% named natural light and windows that open as a priority.

For a small team, that is good news. A pantry where people actually sit, a table by the best window, and plants that are looked after do more for an office day than a reception desk nobody uses. They are also what a visiting client or candidate notices first.

5. Upkeep: how it looks in year three

The office your team sees in year three is the one that matters. Meeting room chairs that scuff the walls, a pantry counter that stains, carpet that shows the route to the coffee machine: each tells people the office is an afterthought. Choose finishes for how they look after heavy use, and keep spares of anything that is hard to match later.

Five questions before your next office move

  1. How many people are in on your busiest day, and how many are on calls at the same time?
  2. How many meetings in your calendar have two or three people, and how many rooms do you have for them?
  3. Where can someone do two hours of focused work without headphones?
  4. Where does the noise from the entrance and pantry travel?
  5. What would make someone choose to come in on a day they don't have to?
