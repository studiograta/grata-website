Attic stock: the line item nobody budgets

Why one of the cheapest protections in a hotel fit-out is a locked storeroom of your own materials.

17 October 2026

A guest drags a suitcase across the floor of room 214 and cracks a tile. It's an easy fix, until someone opens the supplier's catalogue. The tile has been discontinued, or the new batch is half a shade warmer than the one already on the floor.

Now there are two options. Take the room out of inventory while you hunt for a match, or patch it and live with a floor that looks patched. Either way, the guest in that room is paying for the property's weakest square foot.

That's the problem attic stock solves.

What attic stock is

Attic stock means the spares of every finish in the building. You buy them with the main order, from the same production batch, and store them on site with clear labels. They're not leftovers from the contractor's last truck. They're a planned quantity, bought on purpose.

Why the batch matters

Most materials that go into a hotel are made in lots, and lots drift.

  • Tiles carry shade and calibre codes. Two boxes of the same tile from different runs can differ in colour and in size.
  • Fabrics are dyed in lots. A replacement cushion cover from a later lot can stand out on a sofa that's been in the sun for two years.
  • Stone comes from blocks. A slab from a new block won't carry the same veining as the rest of the lobby.
  • Veneers, laminates and wallcoverings vary from one run to the next and get discontinued without notice.

Reordering later rarely gives you a match. Buying the spares on day one does.

What we keep

For a hotel or resort, our attic stock list usually covers:

  • floor and wall tiles, and stone, for every area
  • upholstery, curtain and headboard fabric
  • carpet and wallcovering
  • laminate and veneer panels for joinery fronts
  • handles, hinges and other ironmongery
  • tap cartridges, shower heads and other wearing parts
  • light fittings and lamps in the specified colour temperature
  • room numbers and signage blanks

How much to keep

There's no single percentage. We size each item by asking three questions:

  1. How does it fail? Floors near entrances, luggage racks and bathroom tiles take the most damage, so they need more spares than a feature wall in the lobby.
  2. How hard is it to replace? Imported, handmade and custom-dyed materials need a bigger buffer than a standard product a local dealer will carry for years.
  3. How long is the lead time? If a reorder takes twelve weeks, every week you wait is a week with a room that's out of order or patched.

As a starting point, we keep:

  • Tiles and stone: about 5% of the laid area, rising to 10% at entrances, corridors and bathrooms
  • Fabric: about 10% of each upholstery and curtain fabric, enough to re-cover at least one full piece of every type
  • Carpet: 3 to 5% of the area, at the top of that range for corridors

Imported and handmade materials sit above these figures.

Where it goes wrong

Attic stock usually fails in the same few ways:

  • The spares get used up during snagging, before the hotel opens.
  • They're stored in a damp basement, and the cartons rot.
  • No one labels them, so the housekeeping team can't tell which spare tile belongs to which floor.
  • The contractor takes them away at handover.

The fix is simple. Treat attic stock as part of handover, with a register that maps each item to the rooms and areas it belongs to, and hand that register to the operator along with the keys.

What it costs, and what it saves

Attic stock is a small slice of the materials budget. What it protects is the number that matters most to an operator over time: upkeep. A room that stays in inventory, looks the way it did on opening day and can be repaired in hours instead of weeks keeps earning.

That's why we put attic stock into the specification at the design stage, not as an afterthought at handover. It sits with our freeze points: once a material is locked for ordering, the spare quantity is locked with it.

Five questions to ask your designer

  1. Is attic stock in the specification and the bill of quantities?
  2. Will the spares come from the same batch as the main order?
  3. Who stores them, where, and in what conditions?
  4. Is there a register that maps spares to rooms?
  5. Which materials on this project are hardest to replace, and how big is their buffer?

If your designer has clear answers to these, your rooms will hold up long after opening.
