The Four Numbers: what your room earns per seat, per hour

Founders budget a fit-out per square foot. The room pays it back per seat, per hour.

date
2 October 2026
read
6 min

Shirin KC

Every fit-out conversation starts with cost per square foot. It's the wrong number to design around. Your rent is charged by the square foot, but your revenue is earned by the seat, by the hour.

Cornell's Sheryl Kimes gave restaurants a way to measure this: RevPASH, revenue per available seat hour. It works the same way hotels use revenue per available room. Roughly:

RevPASH seat occupancy × spend per cover ÷ how long each cover stays

Every one of those terms is shaped by the room. That's why we brief every project on four numbers: capacity, value, use and upkeep. Here is what each one does to your revenue, and the design decisions behind it.

1. Capacity: a full room can be half empty

Picture a Friday night. Every table is taken and there's a queue at the door. It looks like a great night.

Now count chairs instead of tables. If the floor is all four-tops and most groups are couples, half your seats are empty while the room looks full. The host is turning people away from a room running at 50% seat occupancy.

This happens a lot. Kimes studied a Chevys restaurant near Phoenix whose tables were mostly four-tops, while most guests came alone or in pairs. The restaurant changed its table mix, retrained servers and tightened how bills were settled. The extra revenue from higher occupancy paid back the capital cost within a year, without raising menu prices.

How to fix it depends on size. A Cornell simulation by Gary Thompson found that small rooms (around 50 seats) with mostly small parties did best with small tables that combine. Larger rooms (around 200 seats) with big walk-in groups did better with dedicated tables sized for each party, because pushing tables together leaves seats idle while you wait for neighbours to finish.

The design question: not “how many seats fit?” but “how many seats get used, by the groups who actually come?” We start from your POS party-size data, then set the table mix, which tables join, and where.

2. Value: spend per cover is partly a layout decision

Spend per cover depends on the menu, and also on where people sit and what they can see while they wait.

  • A queue on the pavement earns nothing. The same queue at a bar rail buys drinks.
  • A counter seat facing the kitchen sells the dish the next person orders.
  • A dessert or bakery display on the route to the washroom gets seen twice.

The design question: where in this room does a guest decide to spend more, and have we put that moment in their path?

3. Use: the minutes between guests

Here's the term most founders never measure: the time a table sits empty between one party leaving and the next sitting down. Every minute of it is a seat earning nothing.

Some of that is service, but much of it is layout:

  • how far the bussing station is from the table
  • whether staff can clear without cutting through the queue
  • whether the bill is settled at the table or at a counter across the room

Kimes's work names settling the bill quickly as one of the main levers on table time. That's partly a staffing decision and partly a design one.

Use also covers the whole day. A room that's a café at 9 am and a bar at 9 pm earns from twice as many seat hours, but only if the switch takes minutes, not a carpenter: loose furniture, lighting scenes set for each part of the day, storage for what changes over.

The design question: how many minutes does this room lose between guests, and between shifts?

4. Upkeep: count it in lost seat hours

Upkeep is usually budgeted as a maintenance line. Measure it in seat hours instead.

A 60-seat room open 12 hours has 720 seat hours a day. A finish that needs the room closed for a day to repair costs all 720, plus the repair. A banquette that can only be re-covered off site leaves a section closed for a week.

The design question: for each finish, how long until it wears, and can it be fixed overnight? Replaceable edges, kick plates you can unscrew, and fabrics chosen for how they look after 18 months of service all keep seat hours open.

A worked example

A 60-seat café-bar. Spend per cover ₹900, guests stay an average of 1.5 hours, and the floor is busy but runs at 60% seat occupancy because the table mix doesn't match the groups:

  • 60% × ₹900 ÷ 1.5 = ₹360 per seat hour

Fix the table mix so the same busy evening seats 75%:

  • 75% × ₹900 ÷ 1.5 = ₹450 per seat hour

That's 25% more revenue from the same room, the same menu and the same prices. The only change is where the tables go.

Five questions to ask any designer, including us

  1. What is the seat count at our real party mix, not the maximum?
  2. What happens on a Friday when a group of six walks in?
  3. Where does the queue stand, and does it earn?
  4. How many steps from each table to the nearest station?
  5. Which finishes would force us to close to repair them?

If a designer can't answer these before showing you a moodboard, the room has been designed for opening night.

Not a restaurant?

The same logic applies under different names. Hotels count room nights, spas count treatment hours, studios count class slots. In each case the question is the same: what does each unit of space earn per hour, and what is the room doing to that number?
