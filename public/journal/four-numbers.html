The Four Numbers: how we judge a space before we draw it

Many rooms are designed for opening day. Your business runs on every day after.

A new restaurant looks its best on the night it opens. The lights are tuned, the paint is fresh, and every table is full of friends. Six months later it is a Tuesday lunch, half the seats are empty, a bar front is scuffed, and the team is resetting the room by hand before the evening crowd arrives.

We design for that Tuesday. Before we draw anything, we ask four questions. Each one comes from how the business actually runs.

1. Capacity

How many people can this floor hold before it feels crowded?

Maximum seats is the wrong target. The right target is the number of guests the room can hold comfortably, in the group sizes that actually turn up. If most Friday tables are twos, a floor of fixed fours wastes chairs every night.

Example: 48% of Friday groups are twos. In the room: tables for two that join into fours, so the same floor works for a date night and a birthday.

2. Value

What will people pay to be here, and to come back?

Spend varies through the day. People spend more when they feel looked after, and that includes the fifteen minutes they spend waiting for a table.

Example: spend per head at 8 to 10 pm on a Friday is 2.4× lunch. In the room: a bar that serves the queue, so the wait earns drinks revenue instead of losing guests.

3. Use

How hard does the space work, from morning to night?

Many operators run more than one business in one space: a cafe by day and a bar by night, or brunch that turns into dinner. The changeover has to be easy for the staff on duty, not a job for a carpenter.

Example: 20 minutes to go from breakfast to the bar. In the room: loose furniture and preset lighting scenes, so one room can work two shifts.

4. Upkeep

What does it cost to keep it looking like opening day?

Every finish wears. What matters is where it wears, how soon, and whether you can fix it overnight or have to close for a week.

Example: bar fronts start to show wear at 18 months. In the room: replaceable kick pads and edges, so the wear is planned for.

Not a restaurant?

The same four numbers apply under different names. Capacity is rooms, treatment beds or class size. Value is room rate, package add-ons or a membership upgrade. Use is the changeover between guests, treatments or classes. Upkeep is the same everywhere.

Where the numbers come from

On a live project we take the numbers from three places:

  • your POS data
  • your F&B consultant, if you have one
  • our own site walks

We also keep an openings file: notes on seating, lighting and queues from spaces we visit regularly. It lets us compare what still fills on a Tuesday with what only fills on opening night.

A beautiful room that seats the wrong groups is still the wrong room.

What this changes about the process

The numbers are agreed in our first stage, Measure, before any design work. Design and materials are then settled together in Resolve. Since the brief is written in the operator's terms, every design decision has a reason the client can check.

If you are an F&B consultant or PMC

Bring your numbers. We would much rather start from your covers forecast and menu engineering than guess. It saves the client a round of redesign, and it means our drawings match your plan.
