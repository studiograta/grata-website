The view is on the rate card

Guests pay for the setting. The room layout decides how much of it you get to sell.

date
13 October 2026
read
6 min
by
Shirin KC

Every resort brochure leads with the land: the valley at sunrise, the plantation mist, the pool against the hills. The rooms are often planned as if the land were a backdrop, laid out in neat rows to fit the most keys on the plot.

That leaves money on the table. In a study of Mediterranean hotels, rooms with a sea view were priced about 10% higher than rooms without one (Fleischer, 2012). The figure will differ for a hill resort in Coorg or a backwater property in Kerala, but the principle holds. A view is a line on your rate card, and the layout decides how many rooms can charge for it.

Read the land before you draw

A site walk tells you what the place feels like. Satellite photos tell you roughly where things are. Neither tells you enough to plan rooms around.

A detailed site survey does. It shows:

  • Contours and slope: where the land rises and falls, metre by metre
  • Aspect: which way each part of the site faces, and so how much sun it gets
  • Trees: the position and height of every tree
  • Water: where rain runs and collects

With that in hand, the layout stops being guesswork. You can test where each room sits, what it looks at and what it looks over, before anything is drawn in detail. We work from this alongside your architect, landscape designer and surveyor. Our part is turning it into rooms that sell.

Value: count the keys that earn the view

The useful question isn't “does the resort have a view?” It's “how many keys get it?”

Take two layouts of 24 rooms on the same plot. In the first, 10 rooms face the valley. In the second, rooms are stepped down the slope and turned a few degrees, and 18 face it. With a 10% premium for view rooms, the first layout lifts the average room rate by about 4%. The second lifts it by about 7.5%. That difference applies to every night sold, from the same land and the same number of keys.

Then design the view inside the room:

  • The bed faces the window, not the wall.
  • The bath or shower looks out, with privacy designed in.
  • The deck or balcony gets the sunset, not the service road.
  • The bar and restaurant sit where guests will want to be at 6 pm.

Use: sun and slope through the day and the year

The site changes with the hour and the season. A deck that's perfect at sunset can be unusable at noon. A lawn that looks good in March is under water in July.

  • Through the day: which spaces get morning sun for breakfast, shade at noon and the evening light for drinks.
  • Through the year: in much of India, the monsoon is a third of the year. Covered walkways, verandas that still work in the rain, and indoor spaces that open up when it stops let guests use the resort in every season, not just the brochure one.

Upkeep: the site decides how materials age

Which way a surface faces decides how it weathers. West-facing timber takes the harshest afternoon sun. North-facing stone stays damp and grows moss. Anything at the bottom of a slope will see water. On a humid site, every exposed finish is tested every monsoon.

Knowing the aspect and drainage of each spot lets you choose finishes for where they'll actually sit, not for how they look on a moodboard. The result is fewer repairs, fewer rooms out of service, and a property that still looks like its photographs years later.

Keep the trees

Mature trees take decades to grow and an afternoon to cut down. With every tree mapped, “save what we can” becomes a plan. Rooms can be placed between the best trees, decks wrapped around trunks, and paths routed to frame them.

There's a human reason too. In a well-known study published in Science, hospital patients whose windows looked onto trees recovered faster and needed fewer strong painkillers than patients facing a brick wall (Ulrich, 1984). That's hospital research, not hotel data, but it's a strong argument for what a room facing trees does for a guest who has come to rest.

Five things to know about your land before you brief a designer

  1. Which parts of the site have the best views, and from what height?
  2. Which way does each part of the site face?
  3. Where does water run and collect in the monsoon?
  4. Which trees are worth keeping, and where exactly are they?
  5. Where is the sun at breakfast, at noon and at sunset, in both summer and the monsoon?

If your designer starts drawing rooms before these are answered, the layout is fitting keys onto a plot, not selling a setting.
