How a room changes what people do

Guests read the room before they read the menu.

date
6 October 2026
read
6 min
by
Amritha Madhavan

Within a few seconds of walking in, a guest has already decided where to sit, how relaxed to feel and, roughly, how long to stay. They will not remember making those decisions. The room made most of them.

Researchers have studied this for decades. Mary Jo Bitner gave it a name in 1992: the servicescape. Her argument was that the physical setting of a service business shapes how both customers and staff behave and feel. That is the idea behind everything we design. A room is never neutral. It either supports the business model or works against it.

Here is what the research says, organised around the four numbers we use to brief every project.

Capacity: where people choose to sit

People do not spread out evenly across a floor. Jay Appleton's prospect-refuge theory (1975) says we are drawn to places where we can see out (prospect) while feeling protected (refuge). In a restaurant, that means a seat with a wall or a high back behind you and a view of the room. Banquettes, corners and edge tables fill first. The open table in the middle of the floor is usually the last one taken.

Spacing matters too. A Cornell study of a New York fine-dining restaurant found guest satisfaction was highest when tables were at least a metre apart, and dropped noticeably at half a metre. Couples at tightly packed tables left sooner and were more likely to say they would not come back (Robson and Kimes, 2009).

In the room: we plan seat counts for the guests a room can hold comfortably, not the most chairs that fit. We also give centre tables something to anchor them, such as a screen, a planter, a change in floor level or a pool of light, so they do not feel exposed.

Value: how long people stay, and what they spend

How long guests stay depends on the setting, and the right length depends on the business. In an earlier Cornell study of a single restaurant, guests at banquettes stayed longer, while guests at tables in heavy-traffic areas ate faster and spent more per minute (Kimes and Robson, 2004). Neither is better on its own. A wine bar wants people to linger. A busy lunch counter wants tables back. In a spa, the relaxation room after a treatment sets how long a client stays, and how they feel when they leave.

Sound works the same way. In Ronald Milliman's 1986 restaurant study, guests ate faster with fast background music. With slow music they stayed longer and spent significantly more at the bar. Music can also influence what people choose: in a 1997 study published in Nature, French wines outsold German ones in a supermarket when French music played, and the reverse happened with German music. Few shoppers said the music had influenced them (North, Hargreaves and McKendrick).

In the room: we start from the dwell time the operator's model needs, then set the seating, lighting, acoustics and music zones to match it.

Use: the wait, the queue and the changeover

Waiting is part of the experience whether you design for it or not. David Maister's work on the psychology of waiting lines (1985) set out a few simple rules:

  • Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time.
  • Uncertain waits feel longer than known ones.
  • Unfair waits feel longer than equitable ones.

A guest standing at the door with nothing to look at feels the wait. A guest at a bar rail with a drink in hand and a view of the kitchen barely notices it.

This applies beyond food and drink. In a spa or wellness studio, the reception wait sets the mood for the treatment. In a hotel, the check-in queue is the first impression of the stay.

In the room: we give the wait a place. That can be a bar for the queue, a bench with a view of the action, or a visible system so guests can see the order is fair.

A guest with something to watch barely notices the wait.

The threshold: the first few steps inside the door

Retail researcher Paco Underhill described a decompression zone just inside the entrance. People are still adjusting to the light, the sound and the scale, so they tend to walk past whatever is placed there. His work in Why We Buy puts it at roughly the first few metres.

The same holds for a cafe counter, a hotel lobby or a spa reception. A menu board or merchandise wall right at the door often goes unseen.

In the room: we keep the threshold calm, then put the first moment that matters (the menu, the display, the host) just past it, where people are ready to take it in.

Upkeep: what wear tells your guests

Bitner's servicescape argument works in both directions. If the setting signals care, guests expect good service. A scuffed bar front, a chipped table edge or a faded banquette sends the opposite signal, long before anyone tastes the food. Upkeep is not only a maintenance cost. It changes how guests judge the whole place.

In the room: we design the wear in. That means replaceable edges and kick plates, finishes chosen for how they age, and details staff can repair overnight instead of closing for a week.

The room never clocks off

Your staff go home, but the room keeps working: deciding where people sit, how long they stay and whether they come back. The research gives us useful starting points. Most of these studies looked at a single restaurant or store, though, so they show patterns, not guarantees. On every live project we check them against the operator's own data and what we see on site.
