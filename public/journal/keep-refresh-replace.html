Keep, refresh or replace

Most spaces that already earn don't need a new fit-out. They need the right things changed.

A restaurant that was full three years ago now has empty tables on a Friday. The food hasn't changed. The room has. The upholstery is worn at the edges, the lighting feels flat, and a newer place down the road has become the one people photograph.

The first instinct is to start again: strip it out, redesign, reopen. Sometimes that's right. More often, it spends money on things guests never noticed and leaves the real problems in place.

A good renovation starts with a simpler question. What is this room still doing well, and what is costing us?

Start with the numbers, not the mood board

We judge every space by four numbers: capacity, value, use and upkeep. A renovation should move at least one of them. Before anything is drawn, we ask:

  • Capacity: Are there seats or rooms that sell last, or never? Is a corner dead because of how it's planned?
  • Value: Has the average spend or room rate slipped against newer competitors?
  • Use: Is the space busy at one hour and empty for the rest of the day?
  • Upkeep: Which finishes are costing the most to keep looking acceptable?

The answers tell you where the money should go. They usually point to a few parts of the space, not all of it.

Sort everything into three piles

Walk the space with the operator, the head of housekeeping or the head chef, and sort every element into one of three piles.

Keep

Things that work and still look right. Structure, good flooring, solid joinery, well-placed services. Keeping them isn't settling. It frees budget for the changes guests will notice, and it shortens the time you're disrupted.

Refresh

Things that work but look tired. This pile is where renovations earn their keep:

  • re-covering sound seating frames in new fabric
  • re-polishing timber and stone instead of replacing them
  • new wall finishes, from paint to lime plaster
  • new lamps, shades and dimming on existing light points
  • planting, art and soft furnishings

Replace

Things that cost you money every day. Layouts that waste covers, kitchens and bars in the wrong place, finishes that can't be cleaned, services near the end of their life. These are worth spending on, because they change the numbers.

A useful test If a guest wouldn't notice it and your team doesn't complain about it, it probably belongs in the keep pile.

Where the money moves the most

In our experience, a handful of changes do most of the work in an existing space:

  1. Lighting. Warmer, lower and layered light changes how long people stay. It's often the cheapest change with the biggest effect.
  2. The seating plan. Moving tables, adding a banquette or opening up a dead corner can add covers without adding floor area.
  3. Surfaces at touch height. Tabletops, counters, door handles and the first metre of wall are what guests touch and see up close.
  4. The arrival. The entrance, the host stand or the reception desk sets the first impression, and the price guests expect to pay.

A renovation still needs a design

Refreshing piece by piece, without a plan, is how spaces end up looking patched. A new sofa that doesn't sit with the old floor. A bright new light next to a tired ceiling. The keep pile still has to belong to the new design. That means choosing new finishes that work with what stays, not against it.

At Go Native in Koramangala, a renovation of a Charles Correa building shared with Fabindia, the building itself was the thing to keep. The new work, lime plaster, planters and an earthy palette, was chosen to sit with the architecture rather than compete with it.

Where renovations go wrong

  • Hidden services. Opening a wall or ceiling finds old wiring or plumbing that has to be fixed first. Survey before you budget.
  • Mismatched materials. The original tile or fabric is discontinued, so repairs never quite match. This is why we keep attic stock.
  • Approvals. Changes to kitchens, fire exits or listed buildings may need permissions that take longer than the work.
  • Downtime. Every week closed costs revenue. How long the work takes, and whether you can stay open, should shape the design from day one.

Five questions to answer before you call a designer

  1. Which of the four numbers do you most want to move?
  2. What do guests and staff complain about most?
  3. What would you never change, and why?
  4. How long can you afford to be closed, if at all?
  5. Do you have the original drawings and material schedules?

The best renovations aren't the ones that change the most. They're the ones that change the right things.
